\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter states how the system was measured. It is deliberately longer than a
methodology chapter usually is, for a reason the next chapter then demonstrates: on this
task, the measuring apparatus is not a solved problem that can be assumed, and most of
what looked at first like model error turned out to be measurement error. Everything
described here was fixed before the results in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} were read. Where a decision was made after seeing data, this
chapter says so.

\section{What Is Measured}
\label{sec:method-architecture}

The object of measurement is the Layer~1 pipeline of Chapter~\ref{ch:system-design}: a page
image enters, a validated record leaves. Three stages sit between those points, and the
distinction between them is what most of this chapter exists to preserve.

The \textbf{reading} stage is a \ac{VLM} that receives a rasterised page and emits plain
text. It is given no schema, no field list and no instruction about invoices; it is asked to
transcribe. In the delivered system this is Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct \parencite{qwen3vl}, selected by
the comparison of \S\ref{sec:results-reader}; earlier measurements used granite-docling-258M
\parencite{granitedocling}, and every table drawn from that superseded reader says so. The
\textbf{structuring} stage is a second model, gemma-4-E4B-it \parencite{gemma4e4b}, that receives
that transcript together with a field specification and emits a \ac{JSON} object. The model
is natively multimodal; it is used here on text alone and is never shown the page, which is
what makes the transcript the only channel between the stages. The
\textbf{validate-and-repair} stage is deterministic code: it recovers the record from
imperfectly formed model output, matches emitted keys to the field registry, canonicalises
each value by its declared type, and leaves a field absent when it cannot be parsed
(\S\ref{sec:design-validation}). No model runs in the third stage, which matters when
attributing an error --- a defect there is a program defect, reproducible and fixable, not a
sampling artefact.

Two properties of the pipeline are contractual, not incidental, and both affect how it
must be scored.

\textbf{The honest null.} A field the document does not carry must come back absent, not
guessed --- the contract of \S\ref{sec:design-honest-null}, with the professional
justification given there. What matters for this chapter is its scoring consequence: the
evaluation treats absence as a first-class outcome instead of ignoring it, and reports the
rate at which the system emits values for fields that do not exist as a metric in its own
right.

\textbf{Determinism.} Every generation reported in this thesis uses greedy decoding. No
temperature, no sampling, no self-consistency voting over multiple samples. This costs some
accuracy that sampling might have recovered, and it buys the property that a reported number
is a property of the model and the input, not of a random seed. It also means the
stored generations can be re-scored later --- a capability that Chapter~\ref{ch:measurement-validity}
depends on entirely.

\subsection{A synthetic corpus with exact ground truth}

The first is built from the public reference corpus for German hybrid electronic invoices
\parencite{zugferdcorpus}, supplemented with programmatically generated documents. Its composition
and its split are given in Table~\ref{tab:corpus-composition}.

The reason this corpus exists is not scale. It is that a hybrid electronic invoice carries the
same content twice: once as a page a human reads, and once as an embedded machine-readable
representation of the same facts, conforming to the European standard \ac{EN}~16931
\parencite{en16931}. That redundancy is the methodological device this thesis is built on, and
\S\ref{sec:method-gt} describes how it is used.

The documents span the profile range of the standard, from the minimal profile through the
core \ac{EN}~16931 profile to extended profiles, and include the awkward cases that a corpus
assembled for convenience would omit: credit notes, reverse-charge and intra-community
supplies, invoices carrying two different tax rates, early-payment discount terms, foreign
currency, multi-page documents, and a French-language credit note. Several documents are
deliberately defective --- the reference corpus includes a directory of invoices that fail
validation --- and those turned out to matter, because they are where the answer key itself
proved unreliable.

\textbf{Stated plainly as a limitation.} These are born-digital renders. The page is
generated from the same data that produces the reference, so it is clean: no skew, no
shadow, no compression artefact, no camera. A system evaluated only here would be evaluated
only on the easy half of the problem. That is precisely why the second corpus exists.

\subsection{A held-out corpus of real documents}

The second corpus is 39 real invoices collected from the author's own business
correspondence, recorded as a datasheet in the manner \textcite{gebru2018datasheets}
propose and reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:heldout}. It spans two
languages and, more importantly, two acquisition channels: documents that arrived as
email-native \ac{PDF} attachments, and paper documents captured with a phone scanning
application --- camera captures that the application deskews and contrast-enhances into a
\ac{PDF} without a text layer. That is a \emph{milder} degradation than a raw photograph,
which matters when the per-channel gap of \S\ref{sec:results-heldout} is interpreted. Its
per-channel composition is part of Table~\ref{tab:heldout-by-channel}.

This corpus is small, and no claim in this thesis pretends otherwise. What it provides is not
statistical power but \emph{external validity on the dimension that matters}: whether
performance measured on generated pages survives contact with documents produced by other
people's systems and captured under conditions the developer did not control. It was sealed
before use, never consulted during prompt design, and never used for any fitting or selection
decision.

Its ground truth could not come from embedded data, because real invoices largely do not carry
any. Obtaining a defensible answer key for it was the single most expensive methodological
step in the project, and \S\ref{sec:method-heldout-gt} describes it.

\section{Ground Truth Without Manual Labelling}
\label{sec:method-gt}

For the synthetic corpus, the reference data is extracted, not annotated.

The procedure is: take a hybrid invoice; render its visual page to a raster image at
300\,\ac{DPI}; independently extract its embedded structured representation; discard the
embedded representation from everything the models will see; and use it as the reference
against which the models' output is scored. The models receive pixels. The reference comes
from a channel they have no access to.

This buys three properties that manual annotation cannot.

\textbf{It is exact.} The reference is not a human's reading of the page; it is the data the
page was generated from. There is no annotator disagreement to quantify because there is no
annotator.

\textbf{It scales without cost.} Every document in the corpus is fully labelled across every
field the standard defines, including line-item tables that would be prohibitively expensive
to annotate by hand. This is why line-item extraction can be measured at all in this thesis.

\textbf{It is independently checkable.} The extraction is performed with one library and
cross-checked with a second, structurally unrelated implementation --- a Python reference
implementation of the standard \parencite{facturxpython} and an independent Java validator and
extractor \parencite{mustangproject}. Two tools that disagree about what a document says indicate a
defect in one of them, and that disagreement is observable, not silent.

The extracted values are mapped onto the standard's business-term identifiers, so that a field
in this thesis's schema corresponds to a named term in \ac{EN}~16931, not to an
ad-hoc label \parencite{zugferdanhang}. That mapping is what makes the evaluated field set
defensible to a practitioner: the fields are the ones the standard says an invoice has.

\textbf{The critical caveat, stated here and revisited in
Chapter~\ref{ch:measurement-validity}.} An exact reference is not the same as a fair
comparison. The reference stores canonical values; the page prints human-readable ones. The
reference may record a date as an ordered numeric string while the page prints it in German
day-first notation, or store a controlled-vocabulary code where the page prints a phrase.
Comparing a model's reading of the page against the canonical form by string equality
punishes correct answers. That gap between exactness and fairness is the subject of the next
chapter, and it consumed more effort than any modelling decision in this project.

\section{Ground Truth for Real Documents}
\label{sec:method-heldout-gt}

Real invoices carry no embedded reference, so the held-out corpus needed an answer key built
from scratch. The first attempt at one produced a number that had to be retracted, and the
recovery from that failure is itself a methodological result.

\subsection{Why the first attempt failed}

The initial key was drafted automatically from each document's embedded text layer. For
email-native \ac{PDF} files that is a reasonable starting point. For phone scans it is not:
a scanned capture has no text layer, so the drafter had almost nothing to read and produced
values that are not on the page at all. Adjudicating one phone-scanned till receipt against
its own image found a wrong month, a total that was neither the net nor the gross amount but
a blend of the two, two values recorded as absent that are clearly printed, and an address
with a digit missing.

Scoring against that key produced a figure that measured the key, not the system. It
was retracted in the same working session in which it was produced. The relevant lesson is
not that an automated draft is unreliable --- that is unsurprising --- but that
\textbf{the failure was invisible in the aggregate}. The number looked like a plausible
result for a hard task. It was only caught by opening individual documents and reading them.

\subsection{Three independent channels and an adjudication rule}

Building the replacement key was the single largest data-engineering effort in this
project: 1{,}326 cells were adjudicated across three reading channels and a
deterministic gate, under the provenance discipline that Figure~\ref{fig:gt-adjudication}
summarises and this section specifies. The key is built from three channels that fail
differently:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item the original text-layer draft, retained as one voice;
  \item a frontier cloud vision model reading the page images, schema-constrained to the
        field registry;
  \item a commercial document-understanding service with a purpose-trained invoice model
        \parencite{azuredocintelligence}, reading the same page images.
\end{enumerate}

Independence is the property being bought, not accuracy. Agreement between two systems that
share a failure mode is worth very little; agreement between a generalist vision model and a
purpose-built \ac{OCR}-plus-field-model stack is worth considerably more, because the
architectures have little reason to be wrong in the same way.

Alongside the three channels, a deterministic gate asks a narrower question of every asserted
value: \emph{is this string printed in the document's own embedded characters?} That gate
proves presence. It cannot prove assignment --- a seller's and a buyer's tax identifier are
both printed, so filing one as the other passes --- and it can prove nothing at all for the
phone-scanned documents, which have no embedded characters. Both limits are structural and were
recorded, not worked around.

Every cell then falls into one of five provenance classes, and only some of them are accepted
without the author looking:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{proven by printed text} --- the value is in the document's own characters
        \emph{and} at least two channels assign it to this field; accepted;
  \item \textbf{agreed by two channels} --- two independent channels produced the same value
        with no deterministic backing; accepted, and reported as a visibly weaker class,
        because two cloud systems can be wrong together;
  \item \textbf{claimed absent} --- every channel that could express the field reported
        absence; accepted;
  \item \textbf{exempt by policy} --- a controlled-vocabulary value the page never prints in
        the form the schema stores; escalated;
  \item \textbf{decided by the author} --- nothing settles it; escalated.
\end{itemize}

Escalated cells are ranked by how much damage an unnoticed error would do, so that attention
goes to the dangerous ones first, and each is then adjudicated by the author against the page
image.

The outcome: of 1{,}326 cells, 463 were accepted on a recorded warrant and 248 were decided
by hand; the remainder are absences. All 39 documents were signed off individually. The
verification flag is set only by author review --- a frontier model is a better drafter, not a
substitute for a human, and this thesis does not claim otherwise.

\textbf{Circularity check.} None of the three drafting channels is the system under test. The
system is a local vision model feeding a local language model; the key was drafted by a cloud
vision model, a commercial service and a text-layer parser, then adjudicated by the author. No
output of the evaluated pipeline ever entered the key. Automatic acceptance additionally
required either printed-text proof or two independent channels agreeing, so no single channel
determined a cell alone.

\textbf{The privacy cost.} Two of the three channels are cloud
services, so the content of the held-out invoices left local hardware. This cuts directly
against the confidentiality argument that motivates the whole system, and it is acceptable
only under a distinction the thesis must be explicit about: the cloud models are a
\emph{measuring instrument}, never part of the delivered pipeline. Nothing on the inference
path makes a network call. The threat model in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} treats this
honestly, including what it would mean for a practitioner who cannot make that trade even
once.

\textbf{Scope restriction that follows from all this.} The signed-off key covers the flat
header fields. The repeating-group rows were seeded from one channel and never
author-reviewed, because rows do not align across channels --- one reader splits a line
another merges --- so positional comparison manufactures disagreements out of segmentation
rather than out of error. Reviewing them properly would have meant several times the header
workload with no ranking and no warrant. Consequently the held-out claim in this thesis is
about \textbf{header-field extraction}, groups are excluded structurally, not by a
reporting convention, and line-item extraction is measured on the synthetic corpus where the
reference is exact and free. Neither question is dropped; each is answered on the corpus that
can support it. The limitation is recorded in
Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations-future-work} together with the known route to closing it.

\section{The Field Registry}
\label{sec:method-schema}

The evaluated fields are declared once, in a single registry, which simultaneously drives
three things: the specification given to the structuring model, the scoring, and the
reporting. This is an architectural choice with a methodological purpose --- if the prompt and
the scorer carried separate field lists, they could drift apart, and a field could be scored
that the model was never asked for, or asked for and never scored.

The registry defines 34 flat header fields and three repeating groups: line items, the tax
breakdown per rate, and early-payment discount terms. Each flat field carries its
standard business-term identifier, its type, the normalisation rule that applies when
comparing a predicted value to a reference one, and --- where relevant --- the printed labels
under which the value tends to appear on a German invoice. The full registry is reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:registry}.

Two properties of the registry do real methodological work.

\textbf{Normalisation is declared per field, not applied globally.} A tax rate, a date, an
account number and a free-text payment reference require different comparison rules, and a
single global rule would necessarily be wrong for most of them.

\textbf{Normalisation is applied symmetrically.} Any rule that transforms a predicted value
transforms the reference value identically. This sounds obvious and was violated once, with
instructive consequences that Chapter~\ref{ch:measurement-validity} reports: a one-sided
fix intended to make scoring fairer instead scored a correct answer as wrong.

The registry's size is pinned by a test. A change to the field set fails the suite loudly
rather than silently altering what a reported number covers.

\section{The Metric Suite}
\label{sec:method-metrics}

Extraction quality on this task is not one number, and the reason is worth stating before the
definitions.

The obvious metric --- what fraction of fields is exactly right --- fails in two directions at
once. It punishes a model that reads a date correctly but writes it in the page's format
instead of the reference's, and it rewards a model that emits nothing on a document where
most fields are absent. Both failures are well documented in the document-understanding
literature, which is why edit-distance-based measures were introduced for this class of task
\parencite{biten2019anls} and later generalised to handle the structured, partially-absent outputs
that generative models produce \parencite{peer2024anlsstar}. The suite below follows that
tradition, and additionally separates entity-level from group-level correctness in the manner
that \textcite{kieval2025} argue industrial extraction requires.

Every comparison of one predicted field against its reference yields exactly one of five
outcomes:

\begin{description}
  \item[True positive] the reference carries a value and the prediction matches it after
        the field's normalisation rule;
  \item[False positive] the prediction carries a value that is wrong, or carries one where
        the reference has none;
  \item[False negative] the reference carries a value and the prediction is absent or wrong;
  \item[True negative] the reference has no value and the prediction is correctly absent;
  \item[Excluded] the cell is ill-posed and is scored neither way.
\end{description}

The fifth outcome deserves justification, because a category that removes cells from scoring
is exactly the kind of mechanism that can be abused to inflate a result. It exists because
some cells are not answerable from the page at all: a document-level single tax rate is
undefined on an invoice that carries two different rates, and a value that no reading channel
could locate anywhere in the page text cannot be recovered by any vision system.
Chapter~\ref{ch:measurement-validity} states exactly which cells were excluded, on what
recorded warrant, and what the exclusion was worth --- including the discipline that a cell
may only be excluded on a criterion declared in advance and applied by a rule, never
case-by-case after seeing whether the model got it right.

From those outcomes the suite computes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Per-field precision, recall and F$_1$}, pooled over documents. This is the
        diagnostic view: it is what identifies \emph{which} field is failing, and it is how
        every defect in the next chapter was found.
  \item \textbf{Flat micro-F$_1$}, over the header fields of one document, then averaged
        across documents. Each document counts once regardless of how many fields it carries.
        This is the headline figure, because the document is the unit a practitioner cares
        about: the question is how the system behaves on an invoice handed to it.
  \item \textbf{Overall micro-F$_1$}, which additionally pools repeating-group cells. Where
        groups are out of scope this equals the flat figure by construction, so the two cannot
        be confused --- a confusion that did occur once, when a single run reported two very
        different numbers under similar names.
  \item \textbf{Cell-pooled F$_1$}, summing all outcomes across the corpus before computing
        one score, so field-dense documents pull harder. It answers a different question ---
        what share of all extracted cells is correct --- and both are reported wherever the
        distinction could mislead.
  \item \textbf{Presence-conditional F$_1$}, restricted to fields the document actually
        carries, which makes a score insensitive to how often fields are absent.
  \item \textbf{Spurious emission rate}, the share of emitted values for fields the document
        does not carry. This is the honest-null contract made measurable, and it is the metric
        on which the fine-tuning result in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} turns.
\end{itemize}

Correct absences are excluded from the F$_1$ family throughout. Including them would make
every score partly a function of how often fields happen to be empty, which is a property of
the corpus, not of the system.

\textbf{On weighting fields by legal importance.} An earlier design weighted fields by their
significance under \S14 \ac{UStG} and \S33 \ac{UStDV}, so that omitting a mandatory invoice
particular would cost more than omitting an optional one. This was not carried through, and
the reason is methodological, not practical: the weights would have been chosen by the
author, and a headline number that depends on an author-chosen weight vector is not
comparable to anything. The unweighted figures are reported instead, and per-field results are
given in full so that a reader who wants to weight them can.

\section{Sealed Splits and Leak Control}
\label{sec:method-splits}

The synthetic corpus is split into a fitting set and a validation set, stratified by invoice
profile so that no profile appears on only one side. The split was fixed before any adaptation
work and recorded with its seed and a content hash for each side, so that a later run can
\emph{prove} it scored the same documents instead of asserting it. Table~\ref{tab:corpus-composition}
gives the composition and the hash.

\input{tables/corpus-composition.tex}

Three guards keep the validation set clean:

\textbf{Prompt design never saw it.} The field specification, including the printed labels
each field tends to appear under, was developed against the fitting set. This matters more
than it sounds: a prompt tuned by watching validation documents is a form of fitting, and it
is a form that leaves no trace in any model weight.

\textbf{Checkpoint selection never saw it.} The adaptation experiments needed a set to select
a checkpoint on. Rather than selecting on the validation set --- which would have made the
reported figure a best-of --- a further slice was carved out of the \emph{fitting} set for
that purpose, disjoint from both the fitting documents and the validation set, and recorded
with its own hash. Selecting on it leaks nothing into the reported score.

\textbf{Prompt-fixable gaps were never handed to the fine-tune.} Before any adaptation, every
field that scored poorly was classified by cause: absent from the input, present but never
named in the specification, or present and named but rejected by a comparison rule. A gap of
the second kind is a prompt defect, and repairing it by fine-tuning would credit the adapter
with a gain that a one-line specification change would have produced. That classification is
reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Reading Quality, Measured Directly}
\label{sec:method-answerability}

Comparing candidate reading models by end-to-end score is expensive and, worse, confounded:
the structuring stage sits between the reader and the number, so a reader can lose on
end-to-end score for reasons that have nothing to do with reading.

Reading quality is therefore also measured directly, by asking a question of the transcript
alone: \textbf{for each reference value, does the transcript contain it at all?} Matching
allows the formatting variants a German invoice actually uses --- day-first and
month-first dates, comma and point decimal separators, spaced and unspaced account numbers,
and identifiers printed with or without their label.

This proxy has a known blind spot, and it is not a small one: a value can be present as a
string yet unusable because the surrounding structure was destroyed. A transcript that
contains every number on the page in scrambled order scores perfectly on this proxy and is
useless to the structuring stage. The proxy is therefore used as one instrument among several
and never as the sole basis for a decision --- and in the one case where it disagreed with
end-to-end score, Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports how the disagreement was resolved rather
than picking the more convenient instrument.

\section{Error Attribution: Three Instruments}
\label{sec:method-attribution}

The central empirical question of this thesis is not \emph{how well does the pipeline score}
but \emph{which stage limits it}. Answering that requires separating two stages that share a
token stream and an architecture, so no architectural boundary does the separating for us.
Three instruments do it instead; the two that attach to the transcript are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.

\textbf{Instrument one: re-score stored predictions by field group.} Predictions are archived,
so they can be re-scored by cluster --- core header fields, extended header fields, line
items, tax breakdown, discount terms --- without running any model. This localises the loss.
It cannot attribute it.

\textbf{Instrument two: classify every miss by whether the value was findable.} For each
individual miss, ask whether the reference value was present in the transcript the structuring
stage received. If it was not, no structuring model could have recovered it, and the error
belongs to reading. If it was, the value was available and the structuring stage failed to
place it. This is applied per miss, never inferred from totals, which is what makes it an
attribution rather than a correlation.

\textbf{Instrument three: replace the transcript with reference-quality text.} The decisive
one. The same structuring model, the same prompt, the same decoding settings, the same
documents --- but the transcript is replaced by text rendered from the reference data. This
removes the reading stage from the measurement entirely and yields an upper bound on the
structuring stage that is independent of reading quality.

All three instruments use matched decoding settings, and instrument three matters enough that
its own correctness had to be established before its output could be trusted: a renderer that
produces text the reference data does not support would make the ceiling wrong in an
unpredictable direction rather than merely optimistic. Chapter~\ref{ch:measurement-validity}
reports the defects found in that renderer and what correcting them cost.

\textbf{The instrument is not a product.} Reference-quality text does not exist for a real
document. An adapter trained on it, or a score measured with it, may never be reported as a
deliverable capability. This thesis uses instrument three exclusively to attribute a
shortfall, and says so at every point where its numbers appear.

\section{Pre-Registration and Honest Reporting}
\label{sec:method-hypotheses}

Analysing data and then choosing which hypothesis to claim is a well-described way of
producing results that do not replicate \parencite{kerr1998}. Two disciplines guard against it
here.

\textbf{Predictions were recorded before the corresponding data existed.} Each experiment
either cites the hypothesis it tests or is labelled exploratory. The register, with
registration dates, is in Appendix~\ref{app:hypotheses}.

\textbf{Decision rules were fixed in advance and, where possible, delegated to code.} The
adaptation study is the clearest case. Before either training run started, the following were
fixed: the recipe, the selection rule (lowest loss on the held-back slice), that the
validation set would be scored exactly once per arm, that comparisons would be made only
against a baseline measured in the same numeric precision, and --- the clause that matters
most --- that \textbf{a null or negative result would be reported as one}. The selection rule
is native trainer configuration, not a hand-applied convention, so the executed rule
cannot drift from the registered rule.

A pre-registered conjunctive rule was also used for the one candidate-model comparison where
a favourable reading was available: all three criteria had to pass for the challenger to
replace the incumbent, with the criteria written down before the comparison ran. It failed on
one criterion, and Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the failure and the resulting decision.

\textbf{Registered but not evaluated.} The scope of this thesis was narrowed during the work,
and several registered hypotheses --- concerning the two upper layers of the architecture and
a comparison against cloud services --- were consequently never tested. They are listed as
such in Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations-future-work}, never quietly dropped from the
register.

\section{Reproducibility and Hardware}
\label{sec:method-repro}

\textbf{Configuration as data.} Every experiment knob --- model identifiers, dataset paths,
seeds, decoding settings, prompt strings, thresholds --- lives in a versioned configuration
file, not in code. Each experiment takes exactly one argument: the path to its configuration.
The configuration is validated against a typed schema \parencite{pydantic} before any model loads,
so a missing or malformed value fails immediately, before a model has been read into
memory.

\textbf{Tracking.} Runs are recorded with their configuration, metrics and artefacts
\parencite{mlflow}, which is what makes a later re-score attributable to a specific earlier run
rather than to a remembered one.

\textbf{Hardware.} All inference reported in this thesis runs on a single Apple M1~Pro laptop
with 16\,\ac{GB} of unified memory, using the platform's own machine-learning framework
\parencite{mlxvlm} with 4-bit quantised weights. That envelope is not incidental --- it is the
deployment claim. A system that requires a data-centre accelerator does not solve the problem
this thesis addresses, because the firm that cannot send documents to a cloud service usually
also cannot buy a rack.

One exception is recorded explicitly. The adaptation experiments exceeded the local memory
envelope: a path-validation run on four examples ran long enough to be abandoned, against a
real training budget several hundred times larger, and the local training code already carried
three separate workarounds for platform ceilings. Training was therefore moved to a rented
\ac{CUDA} accelerator in full precision. This creates a precision mismatch between the trained
adapter and the locally-measured baseline, and that mismatch turned out to be large enough to
invert a conclusion --- which is why re-measuring the baseline in the adapter's own precision
was made a precondition of the study, not an afterthought.
Table~\ref{tab:precision-confound} reports what would have been concluded without it.
Delivered inference remains entirely local; only training moved.
